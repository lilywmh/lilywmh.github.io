Overview

KIN is a mobile app that helps families stay connected across distance when someone they love needs care. Millions of adult children are managing a parent's health from another city — juggling medications, appointments, and the quiet fear of a missed call. Existing tools solve slivers of this: a pill reminder here, a medical-alert pendant there. None were built for the family as a whole.

I led a team of three from problem framing through user research, high-fidelity prototyping, and usability testing. Our goal was a single platform where a caregiver could see, at a glance, that the person they love is okay — and be guided, calmly, when they're not. The heart of the product is a tiered alert system with AI-assisted guidance that scales its urgency to the situation, from a gentle nudge to an emergency escalation.

👨

Key Flows

Three core functions, one caregiving system

KIN's experience is organized around the moments caregivers need most: knowing when something changes, understanding medical information, and getting guided help when they are unsure what to do next.

Flow 1

Alert System

The alert system keeps routine care low-pressure while making urgent moments impossible to miss. Mild and urgent alerts share the same logic: detect the situation, show the right level of urgency, and guide the next action.

Sub-flow A · Mild Alert Missed medication

Scenes 1-2

Maria wakes late

KIN surfaces an overdue medication reminder, lets Maria confirm the dose, and logs the update before the situation becomes stressful.

Scene 3

David stays calm

David receives a mild notification that tells him what happened and why no immediate action is required.

Scenes 4-5

The system learns

KIN turns repeated missed doses into a pattern David can understand, then helps the family adjust reminders and prepare better doctor notes.

Sub-flow B · Urgent Alert Fall detection & escalation

Scene 6

Detection

KIN identifies a possible fall, checks in with Maria, and gives David an early signal without jumping straight to panic.

Scenes 7-8

Escalation

When Maria does not respond, the alert escalates with clearer urgency, an incident summary, and direct actions David can take immediately.

Scenes 9-10

Resolution

After Maria is confirmed safe, KIN closes the incident and recommends a follow-up action to reduce the risk of another fall.

Flow 2 Health report review

Turning test results into clear takeaways

Caregivers can upload or open a health report and let KIN summarize what changed, what needs attention, and what to bring up at the next appointment.

Flow 3 AI chat assistant

Asking follow-up questions in plain language

After reviewing a report, caregivers can ask KIN's chat assistant what a result means, what to monitor, and how to prepare for a conversation with a clinician.

Usability Testing

Testing the high-blood-pressure alert flow

We ran a moderated usability study (TAC-310) to answer one question: can caregivers understand and respond to a high-blood-pressure alert inside KIN? We combined a 5-second first-impression test with a walkthrough of the notification flow.

Purpose

Assess whether caregivers can effectively understand and respond to a high-BP alert within the app.

Goal

Evaluate whether they can recognize the urgency of the situation and determine appropriate next steps.

Hypothesis

Caregivers will quickly recognize the alert as urgent and respond appropriately with minimal difficulty.

Participants · N = 8

5 / 3female / male · 6 aged 18–24, 2 aged 35–44
6 of 8strongly comfortable using smartphone apps
5 of 8had no current caregiving involvement

5-Second Test

  • ›Most identified KIN as a health-monitoring and caregiving tool, but some were unclear on its exact purpose and audience.
  • ›Clarity ratings were moderate-to-high; the screen's core message came across.
  • ›Personal relevance was low for over half — reflecting a young, healthy sample with no caregiving experience.

Notification Flow

  • ›Most understood the flow as a health alert prompting them to check on someone or take action.
  • ›No major navigation confusion; usability rated highly — all participants gave 4 or 5 for ease of understanding.
  • ›7 of 8 found the alert urgent, rating it 4 or 5.

Reflection

What we learned, and what changed

1 · The value proposition needed to be sharper

Users grasped the product in general but were uncertain about its purpose and audience. We refined the value proposition and onboarding messaging to state more clearly what KIN does and who it's for.

2 · The main screen's hierarchy could be stronger

Clarity of the home screen was only moderate (most ratings 3–4). We simplified and strengthened key UI elements — headlines, labels, and visual hierarchy — to make the product's function immediately legible.

3 · Alerts needed to feel different from one another

Users found the flow intuitive but couldn't tell alert types apart. This led to the design's biggest evolution: a tiered notification system — routine reminders, health warnings, and emergency escalations — each with distinct visuals, defined action items, and a clear level of KIN involvement at every level.
