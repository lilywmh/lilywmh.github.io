Is connection about how we talk, not what we say?

Linguistic, vocal, and visual coordination as correlates of social connection in dyadic co-viewing — measuring all three channels in the same conversations to ask which layer actually carries the feeling.

Background

Shared experiences bond us — but which layer carries the feeling?

People coordinate a conversation almost effortlessly: they take turns with sub-second precision, reuse each other's words, drift toward each other's pitch and loudness, and orient their bodies to one another. A long tradition links this coordination to social bonding — but it's usually measured in one channel per study, in words or voice or body, and the channels are then treated as interchangeable expressions of a single "rapport."

That leaves two open questions. Within language, is connection carried by the content partners share — what they think and say — or by the process of the exchange itself? Shared Reality Theory predicts that similar interpretations should matter; a responsiveness account locates the signal in whether each turn is met and built upon. And across channels: if coordinated dyads simply coordinate everything, channel choice is convenience — but if the channels dissociate, theories and applied systems must specify which one actually matters. Answering that requires measuring all channels in the same conversations, establishing reliability before testing validity.

Research Questions

Three questions, tested in sequence

RQ1 · Before talking

Does pre-discussion interpretive alignment — how similarly partners understood the film, from their written reflections — predict connection?

RQ2 · Content vs. process

Within the discussion, is connection associated with content similarity or with interaction dynamics?

RQ3 · Which channel

Across channels, does vocal or visual coordination add variance beyond the linguistic baseline?

Three Channels

Three lenses on one conversation

The same 24 dyads — strangers who watched Piper and Partly Cloudy, wrote reflections, and discussed each film — are analyzed through three coordinated channels measured from the identical sessions. Two comparable conversations per dyad let every measure be checked for cross-session reliability before any outcome test.

01

Channel · Content vs. process

Linguistic — What They Say vs. How They Say It

The linguistic channel splits cleanly in two. Content is what partners independently thought and said — the alignment of their pre-discussion reflections and the semantic/sentiment overlap of their transcripts. Process is how the conversation actually unfolded — ten interactional moves coded turn by turn. Measuring both lets content and process compete directly.

Strangers wore fNIRS caps throughout (neural data not analyzed here). A final questionnaire captured four connection outcomes — enjoyment, co-viewing preference, shared reality, and partner evaluation; the primary connection composite is the equal-weight mean of the last three (Cronbach's α = .72; r = 1.00 with their first principal component).

Content — what they say

Semantic similarity (SBERT), sentiment alignment (RoBERTa), and dictionary-based affective alignment — computed on both the pre-discussion reflections and the discussion transcripts. Reflections index what partners independently thought, cleanly separated from how they later talked.

Process — how they interact

Structural features (turn-taking, participation balance) plus ten interactional dimensions coded turn by turn by a large language model — agreement type, question-asking, epistemic stance, convergence — yielding measures like elaborated agreement and question asymmetry.

02

Channel · Adds signal

Vocal — Prosodic Entrainment

If how partners interact matters more than what they say, the voice is the next place to look. This channel tests whether vocal coordination at turn transitions is distinct from the transcript dynamics and adds its own signal to connection — the key test being incremental variance beyond the linguistic baseline, not correlation on its own.

Method in brief

Discussions were recorded on a single shared microphone, so coordination is measured at speaker transitions (turn-adjacent), not as continuous overlap. Speaker turns were manually labeled; WhisperX supplied word timing (95% coverage). With Praat/Parselmouth I extracted f0, intensity, and speaking rate per turn — plus 500 ms turn-edge windows — with f0 and intensity z-scored within speaker so coordination reflects relative contour, not baseline voice or mic distance.

A distinct dimension

Vocal and transcript composites were essentially uncorrelated (r = .03–.21, p > .37) — vocal alignment indexes coordination largely separate from turn-level conversational dynamics.

Real entrainment, and loudness carries it

Against the pseudo-dyad null, real dyads showed above-chance synchrony for pitch (real r = .19 vs. .01, p = .024) and intensity (.24 vs. .00, p = .024) but not rate. In the hierarchical models the linguistic composite + order explained R² = .26; the blunt omnibus vocal composite added essentially nothing (ΔR² = .003, p = .79), while the theory-specified intensity-edge index added ΔR² = .198 (permutation p = .015, partial r = .52). Dyads whose loudness converged at the moment of turn exchange felt more connected — beyond everything the transcripts captured.

Honest caveats

The index was motivated by prior literature but not preregistered for this dataset, so it is exploratory; the increment attenuated when age and gender entered the model, so demographic confounding cannot be excluded at N = 24. An earlier "negative rate" association turned out to be a measurement artifact — a cautionary example that reversed once a validated syllable-rate measure was used.

03

Channel · Reliable, but null

Visual — Behavioral Coordination

The third channel takes the "how, not what" logic to the body: pose estimation on the discussion videos, measuring upper-limb movement, postural openness, and how often partners orient their heads toward each other. A null here would be uninformative if the measures were noisy — so the video variables were selected for reliability alone, before any outcome analysis, guaranteeing the null can't be a selection artifact.

Reliability-first selection

MediaPipe Pose, Hands, and FaceMesh on frames sampled at 2 Hz within turn windows. Candidate variables were screened on detection reliability, cross-session stability (a dyad's two films as test–retest), and interpretability — with outcome associations deliberately not consulted. Four variables passed; unreliable hand-landmark measures and unstable frame-level movement synchrony (r = −.21) and near-face hand rates (r = −.33) were rejected, and reported for transparency.

Behaviorally valid

The battery passed built-in validity checks before it was ever related to connection:

  • ›94% of participants (45 of 48) moved their arms more while speaking than listening.
  • ›Pose detection was at ceiling (> .999) in every session.
  • ›Mutual head orientation was the single most stable measure in any channel (r = .79).

No video variable added meaningful variance beyond the linguistic and vocal predictors — all ΔR² ≤ .017, all permutation p ≥ .45 (FDR-adjusted ≈ .93), unchanged when the vocal index was removed and in a quality-control-gated sensitivity arm. Because the measures were selected for reliability first, this is a real null, not attenuation: reliable, behaviorally valid instruments simply carry no connection-relevant signal beyond the other channels. Caveats remain — seated film discussions restrict gross movement, and head orientation is a proxy rather than calibrated gaze.

Conclusion · a multimodal dissociation

From the same sample and pipeline, the vocal channel adds substantial variance while visible movement adds essentially none — a dissociation, not a matter of measurement quality.

The answer was consistent across two independent contrasts. Within language, content similarity and pre-discussion interpretive alignment predicted nothing, while interaction dynamics — question asymmetry and elaborated agreement — tracked connection. Across channels, vocal coordination at turn exchanges predicted connection beyond the linguistic baseline, while reliable, behaviorally valid video measures added nothing. Connection tracked how partners interacted rather than what they said, and within the how, the auditory channel specifically. This fits an account of shared reality built in the interaction — being met at the level one just spoke at — rather than through similarity of content or visible movement.

What comes next

A preregistered replication with per-speaker audio, calibrated eye-tracking alongside the movement battery, connection measured after each conversation, formal inter-rater validation of the LLM annotation scheme, and a sample sized for effects an order of magnitude smaller than these pilot estimates. The fNIRS data already collected in this paradigm offer a further test: the responsiveness account predicts that vocal and interactional coordination — not content overlap — should track neural coupling between partners.
